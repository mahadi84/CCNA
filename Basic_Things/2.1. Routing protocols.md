

<h4>A routing protocol is a set of rules and communication processes that network routers use to exchange topology and path information so they can automatically select the best route for data packets</h4> <br><br>
How Routing Protocols Work: 1. Discovery-> 2. Sharing-> 3. Building Maps-> 4. Adapting
<br>

Routing protocols are broadly categorized into two main domains: <br> **Interior Gateway Protocols (IGP)** used within a single autonomous(স্বশাসিত) system/network, and <br> **Exterior Gateway Protocols (EGP)** used to connect different autonomous systems across the Internet.

Within these domains, there are **5 primary active routing protocols** used in modern computer networking, categorized by their underlying algorithm:

---

### 1. Distance-Vector Routing Protocols

These protocols calculate the best path based primarily on distance (hop count) and direction (vector). They periodically send their entire routing table to neighboring routers.

* **RIP (Routing Information Protocol):** Uses hop count as its metric (maximum 15 hops). Primarily used in small, simple networks due to slow convergence and scale limits.
* 💡 **Analogy:** **Asking for directions step-by-step.** Like asking strangers at every corner, *"Which way to the post office?"* and taking the path with the fewest turns, without ever looking at a full map.


* **EIGRP (Enhanced Interior Gateway Routing Protocol):** Cisco's proprietary (now open-standard) advanced distance-vector protocol. Uses bandwidth, delay, load, and reliability to calculate metrics with very fast convergence.
* 💡 **Analogy:** **Asking a local expert driver with a radio.** Like getting directions from a delivery driver who not only counts turns, but also factor in speed limits, traffic congestion, and road quality to give you the fastest route.



---

### 2. Link-State Routing Protocols

Instead of sharing full routing tables, link-state protocols advertise the status of their direct links. Each router builds a complete topological map of the network and computes the shortest path using Dijkstra's algorithm.

* **OSPF (Open Shortest Path First):** The most widely deployed interior gateway protocol in enterprise and provider networks. Supports hierarchical area structuring to optimize performance.
* 💡 **Analogy:** **Using Google Maps / GPS.** Everyone downloads the exact same live map of the entire city and independently calculates the absolute quickest route from where they are sitting.


* **IS-IS (Intermediate System to Intermediate System):** Operates at Layer 2 and functions similarly to OSPF. Extensively used in large service provider backbones and data centers.
* 💡 **Analogy:** **A railway control room map.** Functions like GPS, but operates directly on the underlying track infrastructure (Layer 2) for massive industrial rail systems, giving high-speed freight trains (service provider backbones) maximum reliability.



---

### 3. Path-Vector Routing Protocols

Path-vector protocols store full path attributes (a list of autonomous systems passed through) rather than simple hop counts or link metrics, allowing precise policy enforcement and loop prevention across global networks.

* **BGP (Border Gateway Protocol):** The standard Exterior Gateway Protocol that powers the core of the global Internet. It manages how packets are routed across different internet service providers (ISPs) and large organizations.
* 💡 **Analogy:** **International passport stamps & airline flight itineraries.** Like flying internationally where your ticket lists every country/airport you must pass through in order, choosing routes based on border policies, visas, and country partnerships rather than just raw distance.
